# ASD-STE100 rules by section

This file restates ASD-STE100 Issue 9 (2025-01-15), Part 1: 53 rules in nine sections. Rule numbers are Issue 9 numbers. The wording and the examples are ours. For the exact text, get the free official copy from asd-ste100.org.

This file is not affiliated with or endorsed by ASD. The examples show structure. Before you publish strict STE, examine each word in the Dictionary.

Contents: 1. Words · 2. Multi-word nouns · 3. Verbs · 4. Sentences · 5. Procedural writing · 6. Descriptive writing · 7. Safety instructions · 8. Punctuation and word counts · 9. Writing practices · What STE does not regulate

## 1. Words (1.1 to 1.14)

- Use only approved words, technical nouns, and technical verbs (1.1).
- Use an approved word only as its listed part of speech (1.2). Use it only in its approved meaning (1.3) and in the forms that the Dictionary lists (1.4). `test` is a noun, so write "Do a test of the pump", not "Test the pump".
- A technical noun is a word for a thing in one of 22 categories, such as part names, tools, materials, and units. An unapproved word can appear only as a technical noun or as part of one (1.5, 1.6). Do not use a technical noun as a verb: "Apply oil to the hinge", not "Oil the hinge" (1.7).
- Use the technical nouns your company approves. If you must choose one, keep it to three words or fewer (1.8, 1.9). No regional words, slang, or jargon (1.10). Use one technical noun for one item through the whole text (1.11).
- A technical verb is a verb in one of four categories, such as manufacturing processes. Use one only when approved words cannot say the same thing (1.12). Do not use a technical verb as a noun. Its past participle can be an adjective: "the reamed hole" (1.13).
- Use American spelling unless official directives require another (1.14).

## 2. Multi-word nouns (2.1, 2.2)

- A multi-word noun has three words or fewer (2.1).
- For a longer official name, write it in full the first time. After that, use a shorter form or an abbreviation, or hyphenate the words that work as one unit. A hyphenated group counts as one word. Do not hyphenate more than three words (2.2).

Example: "the user authentication token expiry value" becomes "the expiry value of the user authentication token".

## 3. Verbs (3.1 to 3.7)

- Use only the verb forms the Dictionary lists. `be` is limited to `is`, `are`, `was`, and `were` (3.1).
- Six forms are permitted: infinitive, imperative, simple present, simple past, simple future with `will`, and past participle as an adjective. Perfect, progressive, and other complex constructions are not permitted (3.2).
- A past participle used as an adjective is not passive voice. It can come before a noun ("the disconnected cable") or after `be`, `become`, or `stay` ("the valve is closed") (3.3).
- Do not use a modal with `be` and a participle. "You can adjust the gap", not "The gap can be adjusted" (3.4).
- Use an `-ing` form only as a technical noun or as a modifier in one ("welding torch"). The approved `-ing` words are listed in word-choice.md (3.5).
- Use the active voice. In descriptive writing, use the passive only when the agent is unknown (3.6).
- Use a verb for an action, not a noun made from it. "The meter shows 450 ohms", not "The meter gives an indication of 450 ohms" (3.7).

## 4. Sentences (4.1 to 4.5)

- Write short, clear sentences. Procedures use the imperative. Descriptive text has one topic per sentence and no imperative (4.1).
- Do not leave out nouns, verbs, subjects, or articles to make a sentence shorter. Do not use contractions (4.2).
- Vertical lists (4.3):
  - Put a colon before the list.
  - Start each item with an uppercase letter.
  - Put no comma or semicolon after an item.
  - Put a period after the last item.
- Use connecting words such as `and`, `but`, `then`, `thus`, and `as a result` to show how sentences relate (4.4).
- Use articles and `this` or `these` where they apply. Do not use an article in a general statement or before an alphanumeric identifier: "Tag circuit breaker 36L7" (4.5).

## 5. Procedural writing (5.1 to 5.5)

- A procedural sentence has 20 words or fewer. This includes warnings and cautions (5.1).
- Write one instruction per sentence. Two actions can share a sentence only when they happen at the same time: "Remove and discard the seal." A step can have more than one sentence when actions are simultaneous. A step can also have a result sentence (5.2).
- Use the imperative. Use `must` only for safety or an important condition (5.3).
- Put a condition the reader must know first, followed by a comma: "If the pressure is more than 3 bar, open the valve." (5.4)
- A note gives information only. It contains no instruction, requirement, limit, or imperative, and its sentences have 25 words or fewer. Put limits in the step. Test: the procedure must still work when you remove every note (5.5).

## 6. Descriptive writing (6.1 to 6.6)

- Give information gradually, one subject per sentence (6.1).
- Use key words and key phrases, and do not change them (6.2).
- A descriptive sentence has 25 words or fewer (6.3).
- Use paragraphs to group related information (6.4). Give each paragraph one topic and start with the topic sentence (6.5).
- A paragraph has six sentences or fewer (6.6).

## 7. Safety instructions (7.1 to 7.3)

- A warning tells the reader about a risk of injury or death. A caution tells the reader about a risk of damage to objects. When the two risks apply, write a warning. Other signal-word systems (DANGER, NOTICE, ANSI Z535, ISO 3864) are permitted when the content follows the three rules below.
- Identify the risk level with a word or a symbol (7.1).
- Start with a clear command or condition (7.2).
- Explain the risk or the result (7.3).
- Each sentence has 20 words or fewer (5.1).
- STE does not say where a safety instruction goes. S1000D and ANSI practice put it immediately before the step it governs. Follow that practice. A warning that the reader sees after the action has already failed.
- A note is not a safety instruction. A note that describes a risk is a caution or a warning with the wrong label.

```
WARNING: Disconnect the battery before you remove the panel. The terminals have a high voltage that can cause injury.
```

## 8. Punctuation and word counts (8.1 to 8.7)

- Use standard punctuation, except the semicolon (8.1). Use hyphens to join directly related words (8.2).
- Parentheses have seven permitted uses, such as references, item numbers, step identifiers, abbreviations, `test(s)`, explanations, and alternatives (8.3).
- In a vertical list, count the colon as a period. The lead-in and each item are separate sentences, each with the normal limit (8.4).
- Text in parentheses counts as one word in its sentence, and it is also counted as a sentence of its own (8.5).
- Each of these items counts as one word (8.6):
  - A number: "13", "twenty-one"
  - A number with its unit: "10 °C", "20 kg"
  - An abbreviation: "VPN", "10 a.m."
  - An alphanumeric identifier: "No. 1", "36L7"
  - Quoted text, such as a panel legend or a formula
  - A title, heading, placard, or label
  - A proper noun: "United States of America"

  Paragraph and step numbers do not count.
- A hyphenated word counts as one word (8.7).

## 9. Writing practices (9.1 to 9.4) and general recommendations

- When a word-for-word swap does not work, rebuild the sentence (9.1).
- Use each approved word correctly (9.2). For example, `turn` has the meaning "to go around an axis". Write limits with `more than` and `less than`, not with `above` and `below`. `see` refers to the eyes.
- Do not use phrasal verbs, except approved ones such as `put on` and `come on` (9.3).
- Keep the wording consistent (9.4).
- The general recommendations GR-1 to GR-8 are advice, not rules:
  - Keep `that` after `make sure`.
  - Show the primary verb instead of "use X to".
  - Use only dictionary pronouns, and make the referent of `this` clear.
  - Look for false friends.
  - Do not use Latin abbreviations such as `e.g.`, `i.e.`, and `etc.`
  - Do not use `he` or `she`.
  - Use the possessive with care.

## What STE does not regulate

STE does not regulate formatting, uppercase, units of measurement, number style (figures or words), `and/or`, or where a warning goes. Follow the house style for these, and do not present house style as an STE rule.
